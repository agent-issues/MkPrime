# Red-team: is the mk_k40 > mk paradox tree-shape-confounded?

Date: 2026-05-19
Data: `dev/pilots/2026-05-12-prior-validation/analysis/cid_nine_prior.rds`
      (9 arms × 26 trees × 10 reps; mkp_geo has only 1 rep so excluded from
      paired analysis).
Tree shapes: `C:/Users/pjjg18/GitHub/mkprime/tree-inference/trees_meta.csv`
      (`j1_index`, balance: 0 = pectinate, 1 = balanced).
Script: `dev/pilots/2026-05-12-prior-validation/analysis/redteam_stratify.R`

## TL;DR — Verdict

**The paradox is NOT tree-shape-confounded.** mk_k40's advantage over mk is
essentially constant across the J1 spectrum:

- pectinate tercile (9 trees, J1 0.36–0.57): mean Δ = -0.0368 (p = 4.6e-6)
- intermediate     (8 trees, J1 0.58–0.70): mean Δ = -0.0361 (p = 7.8e-6)
- balanced         (9 trees, J1 0.72–0.98): mean Δ = -0.0374 (p = 2.9e-6)

The three tercile means differ by < 0.0013 — well inside one standard error.
mk_k40 wins on **26 of 26 trees** (per-tree mean Δ), and on **97.3 % of 260
individual task-reps**. There is no J1 stratum where the truth-matching
prior catches up.

So the "balanced trees are easy for big k" hypothesis is dead. The advantage
is uniform — which actually makes the paradox sharper, not softer.

## 3. Per-tree paired Δ (`redteam_stratify_2_delta_vs_j1.png`, `_3_pertree_bars.png`)

For every one of the 26 trees, mean(CID_mk_k40) < mean(CID_mk).
Per-tree Δ ranges from -0.057 to -0.022, with no trend in J1 (loess of Δ on
J1 is essentially flat). The CSV
`redteam_stratify_diff_per_tree.csv` lists every tree's Δ sorted by J1.

A few tree-level observations:
- The *largest* mk_k40 advantage (Δ = -0.057) is on tree 14 (J1 = 0.511,
  pectinate-ish), not a balanced tree.
- The *smallest* advantage (Δ = -0.022) is on tree 21 (J1 = 0.78,
  balanced-ish).
- These are individual-tree quirks, not a systematic J1 effect.

## 4. CID(mk) vs CID(mk_k40) scatter (`redteam_stratify_4_scatter.png`)

260 paired task-reps. Almost all points lie below the y = x line, fairly

## 5. Median- vs mean-based ramp (robustness)

Switching from mean-of-means to median-of-medians barely changes the ramp:

| Arm     | mean-of-means | median-of-medians |
|---------|---------------|-------------------|
| mk      | 0.2754        | 0.2764            |
| mk_kp1  | 0.2595        | 0.2650            |
| mk_kp2  | 0.2535        | 0.2551            |
| mk_k9   | 0.2451        | 0.2447            |
| mk_k15  | 0.2418        | 0.2418            |
| mk_k24  | 0.2398        | 0.2382            |
| **mk_k40**  | **0.2386**        | **0.2354**            |
| mkp_eg  | 0.2677        | 0.2709            |

mk_k40 remains the best arm under both summaries. The median ramp actually
*widens* the mk_k40 lead over mk (0.2764 − 0.2354 = 0.041, vs 0.037 under
the mean). So tails are not driving the result — if anything, mean is
slightly *protecting* mk by inflating mk_k40's score via a few hard tasks.

## What the paradox is NOT

- ❌ "Big-k posteriors look balanced and our truth trees happen to be balanced."
  Refuted: mk_k40 wins by the same margin on the pectinate tercile.
- ❌ "Mean is dragged by tails." Refuted: median ramp is the same shape.
- ❌ "A few balanced trees carry the result." Refuted: mk_k40 wins on 26/26.

## Where this leaves the paradox

The advantage of high-k arms over the truth-matching `mk` arm is genuinely
uniform across tree shapes. That means the explanation has to lie in
something other than topology balance:

1. **Per-character likelihood landscape**: high-k may down-weight any single
   character's pull on topology, acting as a regulariser that smooths out
   stochastic per-character noise even when the truth is low-k.
2. **Bias-variance**: the truth-matching `mk` arm gives each character the
   correct k but the *variance* of its k_true draws is large
   (Geom(0.4)+2 has Var ≈ 3.75 around mean 3.5). A fixed high k may sit
   nearer to a Bayes-optimal point estimate than per-character k=k_true.
3. **CID itself** rewards bipartition agreement, and bipartitions are mostly
   determined by tree shape rather than character-level state assignments —
   so character-model details may simply matter less than expected.

Next red-team rounds should probe (1) and (2) directly, e.g. by comparing
arms on character-level marginal likelihood, or by running a mk arm with
k drawn from the *posterior* k distribution per character rather than
k_true. The J1-stratification hypothesis is closed.
